There's a version of London that most people never see. It exists between midnight and four in the morning, when the last tubes have stopped running and the streets belong to a completely different cast of characters.

I walked from Soho to the South Bank last Tuesday. It was cold, but not unbearably so — the kind of cold that makes you walk faster and think clearer.

> The city doesn't sleep. It just changes who it lets in.

## The quiet parts

Leicester Square without the crowds is almost unrecognizable. The neon signs still flash, but they're performing for an audience of maybe twelve people. A man was playing saxophone outside the Odeon. Not busking — there was no case for coins. He was just playing because he wanted to.

Waterloo Bridge at 2 AM is one of the most beautiful places in the world. You can see St Paul's in one direction and the Houses of Parliament in the other, and for a few minutes the entire city feels like it was built just for you.

## The people who stay

The night belongs to the workers, the wanderers, and the people who don't fit neatly into the daytime world. I met a woman outside the National Theatre who was reading a novel by torchlight. She said she preferred reading outside at night because there were fewer distractions.

I didn't ask what she was reading. Some mysteries are better left intact.

## What the dark teaches you

There's something about being in a familiar place at an unfamiliar time that rewires how you think about it. Every building looks different. Every sound carries further. Every person you pass feels like they might have a story worth hearing.

I've started walking at night more often. Not to get anywhere in particular — just to see the city the way it looks when it's not trying to impress anyone.

The best version of any place is the version that exists when nobody's watching.
